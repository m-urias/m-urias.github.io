Empirical Tests of the Heckscher-Ohlin Theory#

The Leontief Paradox#

The HO model’s central prediction is that countries export goods that use their abundant factors intensively. For the United States in the postwar period, this prediction seemed straightforward to test: the US was widely regarded as the most capital-abundant economy in the world, and the model therefore predicted that US exports should be capital-intensive relative to US imports.

The first systematic empirical test was conducted by Wassily Leontief, published in 1953, using input-output tables for the US economy. Input-output analysis allows one to trace the full chain of factor requirements embodied in a unit of output — not just the direct labor and capital used in final assembly, but also the indirect factor content embedded in intermediate inputs. Leontief used this methodology to compute the capital and labor embodied in a representative bundle of US exports and a representative bundle of US imports.

The result was startling: the capital-labor ratio embodied in US imports exceeded that embodied in US exports. The most capital-abundant country in the world was apparently exporting labor-intensive goods and importing capital-intensive ones. This finding — the direct opposite of the HO prediction — became known as the Leontief paradox, and it generated a large literature attempting to explain, qualify, or overturn it.

Proposed Resolutions#

Several explanations were advanced for why Leontief’s results might not, in fact, refute the HO model:

  • Technology differences: the HO model assumes identical production technologies across countries, but if US workers are more productive than foreign workers — due to superior technology or organizational practices — then the effective factor endowment of the US in efficiency units may be less capital-intensive than raw capital stock figures suggest.

  • Human capital: Leontief’s study measured only physical capital and raw labor, ignoring the fact that US workers were far better educated and trained than workers elsewhere. Once human capital is included in the factor endowment, the US may in fact be labor-abundant in the relevant sense.

  • Natural resources: the US imports many resource-intensive goods — oil, raw materials, agricultural products — whose production happens to be capital-intensive. Omitting natural resources as a factor biases the results.

  • Trade policy: the HO model is a model of free trade equilibria, but the US was not engaged in free trade. Tariffs and quotas may have distorted the pattern of trade away from the model’s predictions.

These explanations have merit, but there is a more fundamental issue: Leontief was not actually testing the correct implication of the HO model.

The Correct Test: Leamer (1980)#

The appropriate test of the HO model was formalized by Edward Leamer (1980), who showed that Leontief’s comparison of capital-labor ratios in exports and imports was logically flawed in the presence of a trade imbalance. The correct prediction of the HO model is not about the relative factor intensity of exports versus imports, but about the factor content of net trade relative to factor endowments.

To state this precisely, consider a world with many countries \(i = 1, \ldots, C\), many goods \(j = 1, \ldots, N\), and many factors \(k = 1, \ldots, M\). Let \(A\) be the \((M \times N)\) matrix of factor requirements, where element \(a_{kj}\) denotes the amount of factor \(k\) required to produce one unit of good \(j\). For the two-factor case:

\[\begin{split} A = \begin{pmatrix} a_{1L} & a_{2L} \\ a_{1K} & a_{2K} \end{pmatrix} \end{split}\]

Let \(\mathbf{T}^i\) be the \((N \times 1)\) vector of net exports of country \(i\) (exports minus imports, by good). Then the factor content of net trade for country \(i\) is:

\[F^i = A \mathbf{T}^i\]

where \(F^i_k\) measures how much of factor \(k\) is embodied in country \(i\)’s net exports. The HO model predicts that \(F^i_k > 0\) — a country is a net exporter of the services of factor \(k\) — if and only if country \(i\) is relatively abundant in factor \(k\). This is the correct statement to test empirically, and it differs from Leontief’s comparison in a subtle but important way: Leontief compared ratios within a trade flow, whereas the correct test concerns the net factor content relative to the country’s endowment.

The Heckscher-Ohlin-Vanek Theorem#

The two-country, two-factor HO model delivers a prediction about the direction of trade in goods. With many countries, goods, and factors, the natural generalization shifts the prediction to the factor content of trade. This generalization is due to Vanek (1968) and forms the basis for the main empirical tests of the HO framework.

Setup#

Let \(Y^i\) be the \((N \times 1)\) vector of outputs across industries for country \(i\), and let \(D^i\) be the corresponding vector of demands. The vector of net exports is \(T^i = Y^i - D^i\). The factor content of trade is then:

\[F^i \equiv A(Y^i - D^i) = AT^i\]

where \(A\) is the \((M \times N)\) matrix of factor requirements. The \(k\)-th element \(F^i_k\) measures the net export of factor \(k\)’s services embedded in country \(i\)’s trade: \(F^i_k > 0\) means factor \(k\) is implicitly exported; \(F^i_k < 0\) means it is implicitly imported.

Derivation#

The HOV prediction follows from three assumptions: (i) full employment, (ii) identical and homothetic preferences, and (iii) balanced trade.

Full employment requires that all factor endowments are absorbed in production:

\[V^i = AY^i\]

Identical and homothetic preferences with balanced trade imply that each country’s consumption is proportional to its share \(s^i\) of world GDP:

\[AD^i = s^i AD^w = s^i AY^w = s^i V^w\]

where \(s^i = \text{GDP}^i / \text{GDP}^w\). Substituting into the definition of \(F^i\):

\[F^i = AY^i - AD^i = V^i - s^i V^w\]

Heckscher-Ohlin-Vanek (HOV) Theorem

The factor content of trade in factor \(k\) satisfies \(F^i_k = V^i_k - s^i V^w_k\). In particular, \(F^i_k > 0\) (country \(i\) is a net exporter of factor \(k\)) if and only if factor \(k\) is abundant in the sense that \(V^i_k / V^w_k > s^i\).

Resolving the Leontief Paradox#

The HOV framework clarifies why Leontief’s test was logically flawed. Leontief compared the capital-labor ratio in export goods against that in import goods — a comparison of intensities, not quantities. The HOV theorem makes a prediction about the net factor content of trade, which depends on both the intensity and the scale of trade flows.

In the two-factor case, the HOV predictions for capital and labor are:

\[F_K^i = K^i - s^i K^w, \qquad F_L^i = L^i - s^i L^w\]

If the US is capital-abundant relative to labor — meaning \(K^{US}/K^w > L^{US}/L^w\) — then the correct HOV prediction is:

\[\frac{K^{US}}{L^{US}} > \frac{K^{US} - F_K^{US}}{L^{US} - F_L^{US}}\]

That is, the capital-labor ratio in US production should exceed the capital-labor ratio in US consumption — a statement about net factor flows, not about the intensity of any particular export or import bundle.

Proof. From the HOV conditions and market clearing:

\[K^w = \frac{K^i - F_K^i}{s^i}, \qquad L^w = \frac{L^i - F_L^i}{s^i}\]

Therefore:

\[\frac{K^i}{K^w} = s^i \frac{K^i}{K^i - F_K^i}, \qquad \frac{L^i}{L^w} = s^i \frac{L^i}{L^i - F_L^i}\]

Capital abundance (\(K^i/K^w > L^i/L^w\)) then implies:

\[\frac{K^i}{K^i - F_K^i} > \frac{L^i}{L^i - F_L^i}\]

which is precisely the production-consumption comparison above. \(\square\)

Leontief’s approach compares the \(K/L\) ratio of the export bundle to that of the import bundle. Machinery has \(K/L = 3.0\) and textiles have \(K/L = 6.0\), so the US appears to be exporting the labor-intensive good and importing the capital-intensive good — the Leontief paradox.

The HOV approach instead computes the net factor content of trade. Suppose the US exports $2M of machinery and imports $1M of textiles:

\[\begin{split}T = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad A = \begin{bmatrix} 300 & 600 \\ 100 & 100 \end{bmatrix}, \quad AT = \begin{bmatrix} (2)(300) - (1)(600) \\ (2)(100) - (1)(100) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 100 \end{bmatrix}\end{split}\]

On net, the US is exporting 100 units of labor and zero units of capital — seemingly consistent with Leontief’s finding. But now suppose the volume of trade is larger: $10M of machinery exported and $2M of textiles imported:

\[\begin{split}T = \begin{bmatrix} 10 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad AT = \begin{bmatrix} (10)(300) - (2)(600) \\ (10)(100) - (2)(100) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1800 \\ 800 \end{bmatrix}\end{split}\]

Now the US is a net exporter of both capital and labor, with a capital-to-labor ratio of \(1800/800 = 2.25\) in its net trade. The \(K/L\) ratio of the net export bundle need not equal the \(K/L\) ratio of any individual good. Leontief’s error was to draw inferences about factor abundance from good-level intensity comparisons, while ignoring the scale of trade. The HOV approach, by focusing on net factor content, corrects this.

Empirical Performance of the HOV Model#

The HOV theorem provides a clean empirical test. The prediction is:

\[AT^i = V^i - s^i V^w\]

The left-hand side can be computed from data on trade flows \(T^i\) and technology \(A\); the right-hand side from data on factor endowments. The question is how well the two sides match.

Bowen, Leamer, and Sveikauskas (1987)#

The first comprehensive test of the HOV theorem was conducted by Bowen, Leamer, and Sveikauskas (1987), who extended Leontief’s analysis to 27 countries and 12 factors of production. For each country-factor pair, they tested whether the sign of the measured factor content of trade matched the HOV prediction. The sign test passed in only 61 percent of cases — not much better than random assignment — and the Leontief paradox remained alive in the broader cross-country data.

Trefler (1995)#

Trefler (1995) pushed the analysis further using data on 33 countries and 9 factors, testing both the direction and the magnitude of the HOV predictions. The results were damaging: the sign test was consistent with the HOV prediction in only 50 percent of cases, and the rank test in 60 percent. Trefler also documented a striking pattern he termed the mystery of the missing trade: observed factor trade flows were far smaller in magnitude than the HOV model predicts. Trefler’s diagnostic analysis pointed to the assumption of identical technologies across countries as the primary source of the model’s failure — a finding consistent with the resolution of the Leontief paradox discussed above.

Davis and Weinstein (2001)#

The most influential rehabilitation of the HOV framework was provided by Davis and Weinstein (2001), who showed that the model’s empirical failures were largely attributable to its most restrictive auxiliary assumptions rather than to the core factor-endowments mechanism. Assessment#

The empirical literature on HOV delivers a nuanced verdict. The strict version of the theorem — with identical technologies, costless trade, and full diversification — performs poorly and the Leontief paradox is a genuine anomaly within that framework. But the anomaly is largely resolved once the theorem’s most counterfactual assumptions are relaxed. Factor endowments do appear to shape the factor content of trade in the manner the HO mechanism predicts; the difficulty lies in the auxiliary assumptions required to derive the clean HOV prediction rather than in the core economic logic.

Whether the amended model — with technology differences, trade costs, and incomplete specialization layered on top — should still be called the HO model is a matter of interpretation. What is clear is that factor endowments remain an empirically relevant determinant of trade patterns, even if the strict HOV theorem overstates the precision with which endowments alone can account for observed trade flows.
